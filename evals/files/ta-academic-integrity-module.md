# Module 4: Responding to Suspected Academic Misconduct

*Northbridge University: Teaching Assistant Development Program (fictional institution, created for testing)*

## Why this matters

As a teaching assistant (TA), you are often the first person to notice that something about a student's work doesn't look right. How you respond in the first few days shapes whether the case is handled fairly, both for the student and for the integrity of the course. This module covers what you are responsible for, and what you are not.

## Your role: notice, document, refer

TAs at Northbridge have three responsibilities when they suspect academic misconduct:

1. **Notice.** Common signs include a sudden change in writing style or quality, passages that don't fit the assignment prompt, identical unusual errors across two submissions, citations to sources that don't exist or don't say what is claimed, and code with identical structure and variable names across students.
2. **Document.** Record what you observed, where, and when. Save the original submissions exactly as received (don't edit, annotate, or re-save them), and keep copies of any comparison material. Write factual notes ("Paragraphs 2–4 match Source X word for word"), not conclusions ("The student cheated").
3. **Refer.** Send your documentation to the **course instructor** within **five business days** of noticing the concern. The course instructor decides whether to forward the matter to the **Office of Academic Integrity (OAI)**.

## What TAs must not do

- **Don't confront or accuse the student.** If a student asks why their grade is delayed, say that the instructor is reviewing the submission. Don't discuss your suspicion.
- **Don't assign a penalty**, including a zero, a grade reduction, or a request to resubmit. Only the OAI process can impose a sanction. Grading the work as if nothing happened, or penalising it informally, both bypass the student's right to a fair process.
- **Don't discuss the case** with other students or other TAs who are not involved in grading that work. Concerns are confidential.

## Similarity reports are evidence, not verdicts

Northbridge uses a text-matching tool that produces a **similarity score**. A high score is a reason to look closely, not proof of misconduct. Matches may come from properly quoted and cited material, common phrases, the assignment template, or the student's own earlier draft submitted to the same tool. A low score doesn't rule out misconduct either: paraphrasing tools, contract cheating, and AI-generated text may not produce matches. Always read the matched passages yourself and describe what you see.

## Generative AI

Each course sets its own rules on generative AI, which appear in the course syllabus. If the syllabus permits AI use with acknowledgement, unacknowledged use may be a concern. If the syllabus prohibits it, any use may be. Northbridge does **not** accept AI-detection scores as evidence on their own, because these tools produce false positives, particularly for students writing in an additional language. If you suspect unauthorised AI use, document specific observations (for example, fabricated references, or content that doesn't match class materials) and refer as usual.

## The student's rights

Students suspected of misconduct have the right to be told what the concern is, to see the evidence, and to respond before any decision is made. They are presumed not to have committed misconduct unless the process finds otherwise. That is why your notes must be factual and complete: they may be shown to the student.

## When a student comes to you

Sometimes a student discloses that they made a mistake, for example that they forgot to cite a source or worked too closely with a friend. Thank them for being honest, don't promise any outcome, and tell them you need to pass the information to the course instructor. Then document and refer as above.

## Quick reference

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| You notice a concern | Document factually, save originals, refer to instructor within 5 business days |
| Student asks why the grade is late | "The instructor is reviewing the submission." |
| High similarity score | Read the matches; describe what you see; refer if concerns remain |
| AI-detection score only | Not sufficient evidence; look for specific observations |
| Student discloses a problem | Thank them, promise nothing, refer to instructor |
