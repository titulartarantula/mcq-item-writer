## What to write scenarios about

Adapted from the guide's vignette content principles [NBME p. 39]:

- **Pose decisions, not definitions.** The scenario should set up a judgment the learner will actually have to make.
- **Focus on common situations and on high-consequence ones.** Avoid rare edge cases and trivia that a competent practitioner wouldn't be expected to know.
- **Pitch the task at the learner's level.** Ask for decisions expected of a successful learner at this stage, in situations they will realistically handle.
- **Target the next stage.** Test what the learner must be able to do in their next role, course, or job.
- **Aim where reasoning goes wrong.** Situations where learners commonly make mistakes give the most useful information.

## Scenario template (ordered elements)

Not every element is needed, but **those you include should appear in this order** [NBME p. 39]. A consistent order makes scenarios quicker to write and easier to read, and it makes missing information easier to spot.

| # | Element | Generic form | Clinical form in the guide |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Who** | role, relevant attributes | age, gender (self-identified) |
| 2 | **Where** | setting or context | site of care |
| 3 | **Presenting situation** | the problem, request, or trigger | presenting symptoms |
| 4 | **Time frame** | how long, since when | duration of symptoms |
| 5 | **Background** | relevant history, prior events, constraints | medical, family, psychosocial history |
| 6 | **Observations** | what is seen, heard, or measured now | physical findings |
| 7 | **Data / results** | reports, logs, test results, figures | diagnostic study results |
| 8 | **Actions so far and their effects** | what has been tried and what happened | initial treatment, subsequent findings |
| → | **Lead-in** | one closed question | |

Put all the content **before** the lead-in. Very little, if anything, should follow it [NBME p. 35].

See `people-in-scenarios.md` for when to include personal characteristics in element 1.

## Domain adaptations of the template

**IT support / cybersecurity**
> A help-desk analyst at a 200-person accounting firm (1, 2) receives a call from an employee who cannot open any files on a shared drive (3). The problem started 20 minutes ago (4). The employee clicked a link in an email that appeared to come from the firm's payroll provider earlier that morning (5). File names on the shared drive now end in ".lck", and a text file named README appeared in each folder (6). The endpoint-protection console shows no alerts (7). The employee has restarted the computer once, with no change (8). Which of the following is the most appropriate immediate action?

**Workplace health & safety**
> A new warehouse supervisor (1) at a distribution centre (2) notices a forklift operator reversing without a spotter in an aisle marked for pedestrian traffic (3) during the afternoon shift change (4). The site introduced a one-way traffic plan last month after a near-miss (5)...

**Customer service / communication**
> A customer-support agent for a software subscription service (1, 2) is on a call with a customer who has been charged twice for an annual plan (3) and says this is the third time they have phoned about it this week (4, 5). The customer's voice is raised, and they say they will cancel unless the charge is reversed today (6). The billing system shows the duplicate charge is pending, not settled (7). Which of the following is the most appropriate first response?

**Research methods / statistics**
> A public-health team (1) analysing a city's cycling-injury data (2) finds that helmet users have higher rates of head injury than non-users (3) over a five-year period (4). The data come from emergency-department records only (5, 7)...

## Data-table format

When a scenario has a lot of structured detail, present it as a **labelled table or chart** rather than prose [NBME p. 41]. For example: a project status report, a lab results panel, a server log excerpt, or a financial summary.

**Caution:** tables are poor at showing sequence and change. When timing matters (several events, changes in condition, escalating incidents), use prose or a timeline so the order of events isn't lost [NBME p. 41].

```
PROJECT STATUS — Week 14 of 20
Budget:          spent 78% | planned 65%
Schedule:        3 of 7 milestones complete (planned: 5)
Scope changes:   11 approved change requests (baseline: 2 expected)
Team:            1 senior developer left in Week 10; not replaced
Sponsor note:    "Need the reporting module added before go-live."

Which of the following is the most likely primary cause of the schedule variance?
```

## Length, window dressing, and red herrings

[NBME pp. 44–45]

- Application items legitimately include **some details that aren't needed to answer**. Real situations contain irrelevant information, and sorting relevant from irrelevant is part of the skill being tested.
- **Window dressing** (incidental, realistic detail) is acceptable in moderation, especially for advanced learners.
- **Red herrings** (details designed to mislead) are not. Don't plant false trails.
- If length is a concern, summarise: "The rest of the audit found no other issues" or "Previous quarters were unremarkable."
- Following the template keeps scenarios focused and stops them from becoming verbose.
- Long options are a flaw (ID-LONG-OPTIONS). A long *scenario* is not.

## Basing scenarios on real cases

Real situations are a good source of ideas but a poor template [NBME pp. 33, 45]. Real cases are complicated, and the complications are often not the parts worth assessing. They also tend to contain misleading coincidences. Start from the real case, then **simplify toward the typical presentation**, especially for novice learners. Remove identifying details (see `media.md` on confidentiality).

## Unreliable narrators

Characters in scenarios should be reliable, or the scenario should state the professional's own assessment of their account [NBME p. 45]. In real life, people judge credibility from tone, body language, and other cues that text can't carry.

- ✓ "The contractor reports completing the inspection on Tuesday."
- ✓ "The contractor's account of when the inspection happened has changed twice."
- ✗ "The contractor *claims* to have completed the inspection." This forces the learner to guess whether the writer meant the account to be doubted.

## Checklist

- [ ] The scenario sets up a decision tied to the testing point
- [ ] It covers a common or high-consequence situation pitched at the learner's level
- [ ] Elements appear in template order; all content comes before the lead-in
- [ ] It contains everything needed to reach the key and to make the distractors tempting [NBME p. 69]
- [ ] No red herrings; any window dressing is realistic and limited
- [ ] Narrators are reliable, or the professional's interpretation is stated
- [ ] Sequence-sensitive cases use prose or a timeline, not a table
- [ ] Personal characteristics follow `people-in-scenarios.md`
