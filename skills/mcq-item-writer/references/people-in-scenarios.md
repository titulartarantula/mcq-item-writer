# People in scenarios: characteristics, representation, and bias

Source: generalised from NBME Item-Writing Guide, "Guidelines for Patient Characteristics" [NBME pp. 46–50] and media diversity guidance [NBME p. 62]. The guide addresses patients and health-care staff. This file applies the same reasoning to anyone who appears in a scenario: customers, employees, students, clients, colleagues, members of the public. Examples are original.

## Why this matters

If personal characteristics are worded so that they reinforce stereotypes, or link a group to a behaviour, **validity suffers**. Learners' answers can then depend on things other than the knowledge being tested [NBME p. 46]. Characteristics can also cue the answer, and a pattern of choices across an item bank can misrepresent who the field serves and who works in it.

## What counts as a personal characteristic

Including but not limited to [NBME p. 46]: age, gender identity, sex, sexual orientation, race, ethnicity, country of origin, first language, occupation, military status, socioeconomic status, religion, disability, neurodivergence, family structure.

## Core rules

[NBME p. 46]

1. **Include a characteristic when at least one of these applies:**
   - it is **relevant** to the reasoning (it helps reach the key or makes a distractor plausible), **or**
   - the situation would be **misleading or unreasonably hard** without it, **or**
   - it adds to the **representativeness of the item bank as a whole**. Characteristics should appear often enough across the bank that their presence doesn't signal "this detail matters".
2. **Never include characteristics in a way that reflects a stereotype.**
3. **Report identity characteristics as self-identified.** For example: "a 34-year-old man (self-identified)", or a data-table field "Gender: F, self-identified". Use "unspecified" when a characteristic is withheld on purpose.

## Decision flow

Adapted from the guide's flowchart [NBME pp. 47–48]. The branch structure is our reading of it.

```
Does the phenomenon being tested occur with reasonable frequency across many groups?
├── YES → Is the characteristic needed for the reasoning?
│        ├── YES → Include it, but consider a less commonly depicted group.     (A)
│        └── NO  → Would including it risk stereotyping?
│                 ├── YES → Omit it, or revise the item.                        (B)
│                 └── NO  → Include it.                                         (C)
└── NO (strongly linked to particular groups)
         → Would including the characteristic make the item too easy?
         ├── YES → Omit it, or substitute a less cueing characteristic, and
         │         make sure enough other evidence remains to reach the key.     (D, E)
         └── NO  → Would leaving it out make the item unfairly hard?
                  ├── YES → Include it.                                         (F)
                  └── NO  → Include it for bank-level representativeness.
```

Letters refer to the guide's worked examples A–F [NBME pp. 48–50].

## Worked examples

**Stereotype risk: omit**
> A financial-literacy item describes a single mother from a named ethnic group who "has missed several credit-card payments and relies on payday loans."

The behaviour is tied to a group in a way that reinforces a stereotype, and the group membership adds nothing to the reasoning. Leave ethnicity and family structure unspecified, or choose details unrelated to the behaviour.

**Cueing: change or omit**
> A workplace-safety item about heat stress names the worker's country of origin as one with a very hot climate.

The detail either cues an answer ("they'll be acclimatised") or invites a stereotyped inference. Remove it and let the scenario data (temperature, exertion, hydration, symptoms) carry the reasoning.

**Needed for fairness: include**
> A tax-compliance item asks about obligations for someone who recently moved countries and still earns income from their country of origin.

Country of origin is essential. Without it the item is unanswerable or misleading. Include it.

**Representativeness: include even though it isn't needed**
> A project-management item mentions that "the lead engineer, who uses a wheelchair, joins the site meeting".

This doesn't affect the answer. It is realistic and adds to representation across the bank, as long as it is **not** the only time disability appears and it is never tied to the problem being diagnosed.

## Varying characteristics across an item bank

Across a set or bank of items, deliberately vary [NBME pp. 46–47]:

- **Gender in roles and activities**: who is the engineer, the nurse, the CEO, the caregiver, the athlete, the trades worker.
- **Activities and interests** across socioeconomic and cultural backgrounds, without creating new stereotypes or over-representing activities typical of one group.
- **Gender identity and relationships**: same-sex couples and parents, nonbinary and transgender people, in incidental as well as central roles.
- **Disability**: a range of visible and invisible disabilities, often incidental to the scenario.
- **Caregivers and companions**: fathers, grandparents, nieces, friends, not by default mothers.
- **Occupation and military status**, where realistic.
- **Race, ethnicity, and country of origin**, for representativeness across the bank.
- **Professionals in the scenario**: vary gender and role. In professionalism, ethics, and error scenarios, vary which role made the error.

When generating a batch, track the characteristics used. Report the distribution in the batch summary so a reviewer can check the balance.

## Checklist

- [ ] Each included characteristic meets at least one inclusion reason
- [ ] No characteristic is paired with a stereotyped behaviour or outcome
- [ ] No characteristic gives away the key
- [ ] Leaving a characteristic out doesn't make the item unfair
- [ ] Identity characteristics are marked self-identified, or "unspecified"
- [ ] Across the batch, roles and characteristics are varied and the distribution is reported
