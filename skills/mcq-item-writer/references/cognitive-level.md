## Two cognitive tasks

Items fall into two broad categories by the thinking they require [NBME p. 36]:

| Task | What the learner does | Typical form |
|---|---|---|
| **Recall** | Retrieves a memorised fact, definition, or principle, or recognises something seen before | "Which of the following is the definition of X?" / "Which tool is used for Y?" |
| **Application** | Finds the relevant information in a situation, interprets it, combines it with what they know, and reaches a conclusion, prediction, or decision | Scenario + "Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?" |

Recall items often name the concept and ask for its features. In real practice it works the other way: a situation presents its features, and the professional has to work out the underlying concept [NBME p. 36]. Application items follow that real-world direction.

## Match the level to the purpose and stakes

[NBME pp. 36–37]

| | Formative (practice, feedback, in-module checks) | Summative (grades, certification, progression) |
|---|---|---|
| **Recall** | Fine. Quickly exposes specific gaps; quick to read; suits rapid-fire review and keeping attention in e-learning | Use sparingly. Suits only simple concepts; feels artificial; encourages shallow study |
| **Application** | Supports reasoning, problem-based and team-based learning; gives realistic context | **Preferred.** Closer to real tasks; can test recall and reasoning together. Needs more testing time per item, and it is harder to pinpoint the specific gap |

**Default rule for this skill:**
- `stakes = summative` → write application items by default. Write a recall item only for a simple concept that genuinely has no realistic scenario, and record the reason.
- `stakes = formative` → mix freely. Recall items are fine for knowledge checks, but include application items for the objectives that matter most.

## Turning a recall item into an application item

[NBME p. 38]

**Recall (original example):**
> A retailer's online advertising is mostly reaching people who live outside the areas its stores serve. Which of the following is the most appropriate change to its advertising?

This tests recall. The stem names the problem outright, so the learner only has to remember the standard fix for "ads reaching the wrong area".

**Application:**
> A regional coffee chain increased digital advertising spend by 40% over two quarters. Over the same period, website visits rose 35% and in-store transactions fell 8%. Average transaction value did not change. A customer survey shows that 60% of new website visitors live more than 50 km from any store. Which of the following is the most likely explanation for the decline in in-store transactions?

Now the problem is not named. The learner has to pick out the relevant signals (where the visitors live, transaction value unchanged), interpret them, and work out that the advertising is reaching the wrong audience.

## One scenario, many questions

A good scenario can support several different lead-ins, each testing a different task [NBME p. 38]. From the coffee-chain scenario above:

- **Cause:** Which of the following is the most likely explanation for the decline?
- **Next step in investigation:** Which of the following data would be most useful to obtain next?
- **Action:** Which of the following is the most appropriate change to the advertising strategy?
- **Mechanism / principle:** The mismatch between visits and transactions is best explained by which of the following marketing concepts?

Each later question depends on the learner having understood the situation first. This is useful for building item banks and sequential item sets (see `item-sets.md`).

## What the evidence shows about scenarios

NBME tested the same content in three formats: no scenario, a short scenario, and a longer one [NBME pp. 44–45]. Strong learners did about equally well in all three. Weak learners did progressively worse as the scenario grew, because they had to find and interpret the relevant findings for themselves. **Scenario-based items separate learners who have memorised facts from those who can use them.** That is exactly what a good item should do.

Benefits of application items [NBME p. 38]:
1. They are more authentic, because the learner solves a realistic problem.
2. They tend to focus on important content rather than trivia.
3. They identify learners who know many facts but can't apply them, since those learners must tell relevant information from irrelevant.

## Purpose of the test drives content

Before choosing items, be clear about who is being tested and how the scores will be used [NBME p. 36]:
- A **mid-module formative check** focuses on the objectives just taught and on common misconceptions.
- An **end-of-course summative exam** samples the whole blueprint in proportion to each topic's importance.
- A **certification or competency exam** targets what a newly competent practitioner must be able to do on day one, even if a particular course didn't teach it.

Score reliability depends on sampling widely. A dependable summative score usually needs many items, often 100 or more for high-stakes written exams [NBME p. 9]. Short formative quizzes are useful as feedback but are not reliable enough for decisions about individual learners.
